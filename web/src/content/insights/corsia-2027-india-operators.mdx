CORSIA's next phase, which ICAO's Assembly resolutions set from 2027, widens the scheme's participation basis from a voluntary pilot and first phase to one that draws in a wider set of states and operators. If your airline operates international routes above the scheme's emissions threshold, the practical question is not whether CORSIA applies to you — it is whether your Emission Monitoring Plan and verification arrangements are ready before the reporting year starts, not after.

## What is CORSIA, in one paragraph?

The [Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA) is ICAO's mechanism for capping net CO2 emissions from international flights at a baseline level, requiring operators above a defined threshold to monitor, report and, where applicable, offset emissions growth. IATA maintains a [parallel CORSIA resource](https://www.iata.org/en/programs/sustainability/corsia/) for airlines working through the practicalities of compliance. CORSIA sits alongside — not instead of — an operator's other environmental obligations, including any domestic reporting the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in) sets for its licensed operators.

## What actually changes for Indian operators from 2027?

The headline shift is scope: more states move from voluntary to mandatory participation, so a wider group of Indian carriers' international routes fall inside the scheme's reporting boundary than in the pilot phase. What does not change is the underlying discipline: an operator above the threshold needs a monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) system that a third party can audit, not an internal spreadsheet assembled at year-end.

## Do you already need an Emission Monitoring Plan?

If your international operations sit anywhere near the applicability threshold, yes — an Emission Monitoring Plan (EMP) is the document ICAO's scheme requires before monitoring can be considered compliant, and it has to be approved before the reporting period it covers, not retrofitted afterwards. The EMP sets out your fuel-use monitoring method, your data-management system, and your quality-control procedures, and it is the single most common reason operators fail an audit late rather than build it correctly early.

## Who actually verifies the data?

CORSIA data verification is carried out by an independent third-party verifier accredited to the scheme, working to ISO 14064-3 principles for greenhouse-gas assertions. A lead auditor certified on ISO 14064 and ICAO-recognised for CORSIA verification and validation is doing broadly the same underlying assessment — checking that your reported emissions figure is what your operational data actually supports, not what your plan hoped it would be.

## What is the difference between an EMP and an ISO 14064 inventory?

An EMP is scheme-specific: it exists to satisfy CORSIA's monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) requirements for international aviation emissions, and nothing else. An ISO 14064 greenhouse-gas inventory is broader and standard-based, and can cover an organisation's full emissions footprint, aviation and non-aviation alike. Many operators end up needing both — the EMP because CORSIA requires it directly, and the ISO 14064 inventory because it is what a corporate sustainability disclosure, a lender, or a customer's own supply-chain reporting will ask for.

## What should an Indian operator actually do in the next quarter?

Three things, in order. First, confirm where your international operations sit against the current applicability threshold — do this against ICAO's own current documentation, not last year's briefing. Second, if you do not already have an approved Emission Monitoring Plan, start it now; an EMP that is drafted under time pressure right before a reporting deadline is where most avoidable non-conformities come from. Third, confirm who is doing your verification, and check that they are working to the current CORSIA and ISO 14064 methodology rather than a generic emissions-audit template — the scheme's data requirements are specific, and a generic audit will miss them.

## The practical takeaway

CORSIA compliance is not primarily a legal question — it is a data and process question, and the operators who struggle with it are almost always the ones who treated the Emission Monitoring Plan as paperwork rather than as the actual specification for how their fuel-use data has to be captured. Build the EMP first, verify against it consistently, and the reporting itself becomes routine.
