If you are opening a new terminal, a new runway, or moving an airline's operation between buildings, ORAT is the discipline that gets you there without a first-week incident — and yes, if you have a fixed public opening date, you need it.

## What does ORAT actually stand for?

Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer (sometimes written Operational Readiness, Activation and Transition). It is not a single audit or a checklist; it is a programme that runs for months before a facility opens and covers two overlapping jobs: preparing staff, procedures and systems for the new building, and planning the physical transfer from the old installation to the new one.

The methodology itself is not proprietary to any one consultancy. ICAO's Airport Planning Manual (Doc 9184) and IATA's Airport Development Reference Manual (ADRM) both describe the same underlying discipline, and ACI has published its own operational readiness guidance. What differs between providers is how rigorously the trials programme is scripted and how closely the transfer plan is tied to the actual construction handover — not the name of the framework.

## When should ORAT start — and is T-18 always right?

Eighteen months before opening (T-18) is a reasonable planning assumption for a full programme on a greenfield terminal or a major extension, because it leaves enough runway to write new operational concepts, build training, and run a progressive trials programme before the building has to work for real.

It is not a fixed rule. If your opening date is inside twelve months, the scope has to change — there is no longer room for a full trials programme, and the work becomes risk triage: naming the gaps that would actually hurt on day one, and closing as many of them as the calendar allows. A shorter runway does not mean skipping ORAT; it means being honest with the client about what a compressed programme can and cannot catch.

## What do you actually receive from an ORAT programme?

Six things, broadly, regardless of who delivers it: a master programme with milestones tied to construction and systems handover; a trials and familiarisation plan with scripted scenarios and pass criteria; new standard operating procedures written for the new facility rather than adapted from the old one; a passenger-flow and Level of Service model that drives staffing; an hour-by-hour transfer and cutover runbook, including the point at which you can still turn back; and a day-one command structure that says who decides what during the stabilisation window.

Every one of those is a document or exercise the client's own team owns at handover, and can run again on the next project without the consultancy in the room.

## What happens if you start late, or skip a phase?

The two failure modes are the same everywhere: procedures that were adapted from the old facility instead of written for the new one, and a trials programme that was compressed or cut, so the first time the full system runs together is with real passengers on the opening day. Both are visible immediately, and both are expensive to fix live rather than in rehearsal.

## Who runs the trials — the consultancy, or the airport's own team?

Both, by design. The ORAT team scripts the trial down to the last detail — the staff involved, the flight schedule, the dummy bags and volunteer passengers, and the working of access control, fire and life-safety systems — but it is the airport's own operational teams, together with stakeholders such as customs, immigration and security agencies, fuelling and catering companies, who actually run it. That is deliberate: the point of a trial is that the people who will run the building on day one have already run it once, under supervision, before it matters.

## Where does this fit against an audit or a compliance programme?

In practice, the readiness gaps that surface during ORAT trials are very often compliance gaps first — a procedure that does not meet a safety management system requirement, or an emergency response plan that has not been tested against the new building's actual layout. A transfer programme and an audit programme tend to be run by the same discipline, even when they are scoped as separate engagements.

## The practical takeaway

If you have an opening date, start counting backwards from it now, and be explicit with whoever runs your ORAT programme about how much runway is actually left — that single conversation determines whether you get a full trials programme or a risk-triage exercise, and it is far better to know which one you are buying before the engagement starts.
